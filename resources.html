Safety Planning

Having a plan ready

A safety plan won't cover everything, but having a few things decided ahead of time — before you're in a rush or a crisis — can make leaving faster and safer if you ever need to.

A bag, ready to go

Kept somewhere your abuser won't find it — a trusted friend's house, your car trunk, or at work — rather than packed and visible at home.

  • ID and birth certificates — yours and your children's, or copies if you can't take the originals
  • Some cash, and a spare phone or charger if you have one
  • Any medications you or your kids need
  • A change of clothes for you and each child
  • Copies of important documents: lease, insurance, custody or court paperwork
  • A small comfort item for each child

People and places

Decide ahead of time: two or three people you could call, one place you could go (a friend, family member, or a shelter from our directory), and — if it's useful — a code word you can text or say to signal you need help without spelling it out.

Digital safety

If a device is shared or might be checked, assume it's monitored. Where possible, use a friend's phone, a library computer, or a device your abuser doesn't have access to for anything related to leaving.

  • Clear your browser history after visiting sites like this one — or use the Leave Site button (top right, or press Esc three times) to exit fast
  • Consider whether a shared car could have a GPS tracker — a mechanic or locksmith can do a physical sweep
  • Log out of shared accounts and cloud photo backups that could reveal your location
  • A separate, low-cost prepaid phone kept somewhere private can be a safe way to make calls or texts

For Parents & Children

Bringing kids into the picture

Leaving looks different with children involved, and there are resources built specifically for that.

Shelter policies on children's ages and pets vary by center — many shelters have an age limit for teenage boys because of shared housing, and not all are able to take pets. It's completely normal to ask directly when you call: does this shelter accept boys my son's age? Is there any option for my pet? Advocates expect these questions and can often point you to an alternative if a particular center can't accommodate you.

Your children's right to stay in school

Under the federal McKinney-Vento Act, children displaced by domestic violence — whether you're in a shelter, a motel, or staying with family — have real rights most parents don't know about:

  • Immediate enrollment in a new school, even without the records normally required (immunization records, proof of address, birth certificate) — those can follow later
  • The right to stay enrolled in their current school with transportation provided, if that's determined to be better for them — this can continue through the end of the school year, even after you find permanent housing
  • Every school district has a McKinney-Vento liaison whose job is to help with exactly this — ask the school for them by name

If you're worried about a child's safety

Florida's child abuse and neglect hotline is 1-800-962-2873 (1-800-96-ABUSE), run by the Department of Children and Families. Reaching out for help for yourself and your children as domestic violence victims is not the same thing as a report being made against you.

Counseling for kids

Many of the centers in our directory offer trauma-informed counseling for children, not just adults — ask when you call.

Legal & Immigration Help

Know your legal options

Protective order

Florida's civil "Injunction for Protection Against Domestic Violence" (Fla. Stat. §741.30) can be filed at your county courthouse through the Clerk of Court. It's free — Florida law prohibits charging a filing fee — you're not required to have a lawyer, and you don't need to already have a police report to petition. A shelter advocate can often go with you or help you prepare what to say.

Address confidentiality

Florida's Attorney General's office runs an Address Confidentiality Program that lets survivors use a substitute address so their real address doesn't show up in public records. A shelter advocate or legal aid organization can help you enroll.

If immigration status is part of what's keeping you

Two federal protections exist specifically for this:

  • VAWA self-petition — an abused spouse, child, or parent of a U.S. citizen or green-card holder can apply for immigration status on their own, without the abuser's knowledge or involvement.
  • U-visa — available to victims of certain crimes, including domestic violence, who cooperate with law enforcement.

An immigration attorney or legal aid organization can help figure out which applies to your situation.

Financial & Housing Help

Money and a safe place to live

Florida Crime Victim Compensation is one of the most under-known resources available — many survivors qualify and never apply. It can cover medical and dental bills, mental health counseling, lost wages, funeral costs, and relocation expenses for your safety. You generally need to apply within 1 year of the incident (extensions possible for good cause).

Call 1-800-226-6667, or visit the Florida Attorney General's Crime Victims Services page to apply.

Housing protections

If you live in public housing or use a Section 8 voucher, federal law (VAWA) says you cannot be denied housing or evicted solely because you are a domestic violence victim — and you may qualify for an emergency transfer to a safer unit without having to reapply from scratch.

Beyond emergency shelter

Many centers in our directory also run transitional or rapid-rehousing programs for the months after shelter — ask when you call, even if you're not ready for that step yet.

Building financial independence

When it's safe to do so, a bank account or credit card in your name only can make a real difference. Many shelters also offer, or can refer you to, job training and workforce reentry programs.

FAQ

Questions people are often afraid to ask

Will calling mean the police get involved?
No. Reaching out to a shelter or hotline is confidential and isn't the same as filing a police report. You decide whether and when law enforcement is involved.
Will I lose custody of my kids if I go to a shelter?
No — going to a shelter to protect yourself and your children is not grounds for losing custody. Documented abuse is something Florida family courts consider when making custody decisions.
Is any of this free?
Yes. Shelters, hotlines, protective orders, Crime Victim Compensation, and legal aid listed here are all free.
Can I stay anonymous?
Yes. You don't need to give your real name to call a hotline or ask a shelter general questions, and shelter locations are kept confidential.
What actually happens when I call?
An advocate listens, won't pressure you into a decision, and helps you figure out next steps at your own pace — that might mean information, a safety plan, shelter, or just someone to talk to that day.
For Friends & Family

If someone you love is going through this

A lot of people land on this page not for themselves, but because they're worried about someone else. A few things that genuinely help:

  • Believe them, and don't judge their choices — leaving is often the most dangerous point, not the safest, and there are reasons someone may not be ready to yet
  • Let them set the pace — pushing can push them away from support, not toward it
  • Offer quiet, practical help — holding onto a go-bag, being listed as a safe contact, or just being reachable
  • The hotline numbers on this page are for you too — advocates can talk you through how to help without taking over

This page is a directory and resource guide only — it does not operate any shelter. If you are in immediate danger, call 911. For the official statewide list, visit fcadv.org/centers.
